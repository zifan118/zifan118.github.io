The purpose

What Digital Mirror does

Digital Mirror is a personal academic website built around one question: if the platform shapes who you can be, how much of your digital identity is actually yours? It explores this through three lenses — semiotics, media theory, and intercultural communication — applied first to one person's own digital life, then scaled up to a celebrity case study that makes the same patterns visible at a different size.

"Every platform has an internal grammar — a set of design rules that determines what kinds of expression are even possible within it."

That framing comes from Gee (2005), who developed the concept of the internal grammar of semiotic social spaces — originally to describe digital games, extended here to social media platforms. Kress and van Leeuwen (2001) add the idea that meaning is never made through language alone; images, layout, silence, and the structure of the platform itself all carry meaning. And Adami (2017) shows how the same sign — a blank profile slot, a 4:30am post, an empty caption — can mean entirely different things depending on who is reading, in what space, with what cultural expectations.

This is an academic site, but it is not written only for academics. The concepts are real, the references are real, but the writing is designed for anyone who has ever felt slightly different on different apps — more curated, more casual, more performed — and wondered why.

The author

Who is writing this

My name is Zifan Zeng. I am a Chinese international student studying at the University of Leeds in 2026. I use Xiaohongshu to document and WeChat to stay connected, and I have an Instagram account that I almost never post to. That is not accidental. The platforms ask different things of me, and I have responded differently to each one.

That position — Chinese student, UK context, two platform cultures at once — is not a limitation on this analysis. It is the reason for it. Holliday (1999) argues that what looks like cultural difference is often a question of scale: not "Chinese culture" versus "British culture," but the specific small culture of a secondhand WeChat group, a Xiaohongshu post genre, an Instagram grid aesthetic. Being inside more than one of those at once makes the differences visible in a way they might not be from a single vantage point.

This site is an assessment for MODL2250 Digital Communications Across Cultures at the University of Leeds. It is also a genuine record of what I found when I started paying close attention to something I had been doing every day without thinking about it.

The audience

Who this is for

This site is addressed to anyone who uses social media — which is to say, almost everyone. It does not assume familiarity with academic theory. Where terms like "semiotic resources" or "internal grammar" appear, they are explained in plain language before they are used analytically.

It is written with an international audience in mind. Chinese platforms (WeChat, Xiaohongshu) and UK platforms (Instagram, TikTok) appear side by side throughout, and cultural references — including Chinese text — are always contextualised. The site is also designed for skim-reading: pull-quotes, short sections, and internal links mean you do not need to read everything in sequence to follow the argument.

Kress and van Leeuwen (2001) describe design as the conceptual side of expression — the choices that shape what communication is even possible. Every layout decision on this site, from the dark background to the teal accent, from the Playfair headings to the fragmented log structure, is a designed choice about who is being addressed and how. The site tries to communicate what it is arguing about, through the way it looks.

You might find this site particularly resonant if you are:

An international student navigating more than one digital world Curious about why you behave differently on different apps Someone who has ever felt mis-read online Interested in what platforms do to identity A fan of Justin Bieber, or just curious about how celebrity digital identity works Anyone from any cultural background with a phone and a social media account

A note

What I expected,
and what I found

When I started this project, I thought I was going to write about WeChat. I thought the interesting question was about visual communication — emojis, stickers, Moments posts, the way meaning travels across language barriers through images. That question is still in here, in Logs 1 and 2. But the bigger question revealed itself when I started mapping a single day of my own communication and found that I was behaving like a completely different person in each context.

I expected to find that I chose my platforms. What I found is that they chose me.

That shift — from "I use WeChat" to "WeChat uses me" — is the argument this site is built around. It is not a pessimistic argument. It is just an honest one. Adami (2017) talks about the meaning potential of semiotic resources: the idea that every resource carries histories of use that shape what it can mean and who it can address. When I post on Xiaohongshu, I am not just posting. I am stepping into a genre, a community, a set of expectations about what overseas student life should look like. The same is true on Instagram. The same is true of this website.

What I cannot fully account for is how you, reading this, will interpret it. Meaning is made at the point of reception as much as at the point of production — and that is the final thing this site keeps returning to.
