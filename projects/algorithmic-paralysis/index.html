Algorithmic Paralysis

Rethinking Choice and Discovery on Streaming Platforms

Venue
International Conference of Undergraduate Research (ICUR) 2026, Session 13A, Specialised AI
Format
Spoken presentation; peer-reviewed abstract, accepted May 2026
Method
Walkthrough method on TikTok’s For You feed and Netflix’s TV-show interface
Case
Canal+ Asian, a streaming prototype my team designed

Abstract

Television and streaming content are central to contemporary entertainment culture. With the rise of streaming platforms, content is no longer simply watched but continuously ranked, recommended, and circulated across social media. While viewers appear to have more choices than ever, they increasingly struggle to decide what to watch, often feeling lost within endless streams of similar content.

This study adopts platform and interface analysis, drawing on concepts of platform logic and algorithmic recommendation, to examine how TikTok’s ‘For You’ feed and Netflix’s TV show interface structure user choice. Focusing on content organisation, recommendation flows, and viewing pathways, the analysis shows how platforms prioritise continuous browsing over active selection, reducing exploration and reinforcing repetitive viewing patterns.

These findings suggest that users experience a form of algorithmic paralysis, in which choice is continuously deferred rather than actively made. This shifts media consumption from intentional selection to passive browsing, fundamentally reshaping how user preferences are formed.

This research calls for rethinking how platforms design user choice and content discovery. It points towards alternative approaches that prioritise active exploration over passive recommendation, offering new directions for creating more intentional and engaging media experiences.

The talk

The key points of the spoken presentation, in the order I gave them. The highlighted lines are the ones I want the audience to remember.

Opening about 1 minute

  • An ordinary evening on the sofa: you open Netflix and keep scrolling. Ten minutes go by, then fifteen, and you close the laptop without watching anything.
  • We usually blame ourselves: too picky, too tired, bad attention span.
  • What if the problem isn’t us, but the screen itself?

Research gap and method about 2 minutes

  • Two explanations. Psychology says choice overload, which is about our minds. Platform studies says the filter bubble, which is about what the algorithm does.
  • Neither looks closely at where the two meet: the interface. It is the only place where recommendation algorithms actually reach us.
  • Method. The walkthrough method on TikTok’s For You feed and Netflix’s TV-show page: going through each app step by step, like reading a poster.
  • Three questions. How is the content organised? How do the recommendations flow? What path leads from opening the app to pressing play?

Findings about 2½ minutes

  • 1. Organised around a guess about you. Netflix rows are labelled “Because you watched…”, not by what the library holds. TikTok shows one full-screen video at a time. You can’t explore what you can’t see.
  • 2. Recommendations narrow over time. Every view is fed back in, so the familiar always wins. Scoring eight platforms on ten dimensions, TikTok got 5 for reinforcing watch time and 1 for any non-personalised way to browse.
  • 3. The default path is browsing, not choosing. A preview is already playing. Scrolling on costs nothing. All the effort sits on the side of choosing.

The concept about 40 seconds

  • Algorithmic paralysis: your choice keeps getting pushed back, instead of ever being made.
  • It sounds like choice overload, but it is about the interface, not our brains: the design removes the moment where you decide.

Paralysis doesn’t mean we freeze. We keep scrolling. What’s paralysed isn’t movement. It’s choice.

A case: Canal+ Asian about 3 minutes, with a short video

  • If it was designed, it can be redesigned. Canal+ Asian is a streaming prototype my team made for a D&AD brief.
  • Three ways in. Be surprised, by picking a line of dialogue. Join discussions, and find shows through other viewers’ reactions. Explore stories, from a mood or a cultural idea.
  • Whichever path you take, you start with a decision of your own, not a feed that’s already playing.
  • Testing and limits. Three rounds with small groups, with positive feedback. It is only a prototype: small groups, and no real recommendation system, so nothing yet about scale.
  • Next. Test with more viewers over a longer time. Explore how algorithms could support this idea: explain why something is recommended, or offer a mode that steps outside your usual taste.

Ending about 20 seconds

What kind of media do we want? One that keeps feeding us, or one that helps us explore?
